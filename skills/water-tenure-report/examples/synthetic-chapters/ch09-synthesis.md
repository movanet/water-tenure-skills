# Chapter 9. Synthesis

The catalogue is small, but the patterns that recur in larger assessments are already visible in
it. Each pattern below names the records it rests on.

## 9.1 Layering at a single place

Karangwangi carries eleven records: five primary tenure types, three more as secondary readings, and
one unclassified record. They cover informal pumping and gate opening, customary wells and ritual, a mosque well, unrecognised fishing and sand extraction, a reserve that is not
released, and a disputed water user group (A004, A005, A007, A008, A014, A015, A016, A017, A018, N01,
N03). The type that controls the gate or the pump is often not the type through which people get
their water: the agency schedules the canal, while households take water from wells, hoses and the
river.

## 9.2 Paper water and wet water

The allocation plan sets a 300 l/s minimum release below the weir, and the field officer says it is
not made in the driest months.^[{{S01}} 40; {{INT-AG-01}} l 90-92.] The factory's 25 l/s permit is
on the register, but its use was not observed.^[{{S02}} row 17.] A register is evidence of paper, not
of water; the field is where use is seen.^[{{FAO-PERSP}} 74-81.]

## 9.3 Season decides tenure

The same plots move between arrangements by season: scheme rotation in the first two planting
seasons, river pumping in the third, lessees' pumps in the dry season (A001, A002, A004, A005). The
sacred wells come into heavy use in the dry season (A007).

![Records by the season their arrangement refers to](figures/fig-season.png)

## 9.4 Head and tail

Sukarukun sits at the head of the West secondary with a locked 72-hour turn (A001); Karangwangi's
upland block sits at the tail and pumps from the river (A004). Night-time gate opening at BW-4 is a
tail-end response in the dry season (N01).

## 9.5 Land and water

Land tenure moves water use. A dry-season lease gives outside growers the water along with the land
(A005); household wells are called secure because they are on the household's own land (A006).

## 9.6 Toleration

Two informal uses are known to the agency's officer and left alone as long as nobody complains (A003,
A004).^[{{INT-AG-01}} l 48, 77.] Toleration is reported here as a fact about practice; it is not a
finding about legality.

## 9.7 Invisible users

Women manage household water but none of the 4 interviewed in Sukarukun attends the water user
group's meeting (A002, A006). Women collect from the sacred wells and carry river water when the
canal closes (A007, A017). Fishers are not asked when water is allocated (A014), and duck herders
appear only through hearsay (A013).

## 9.8 The legal-referral agenda

Chapters 3 to 8 list ten questions for legal assessment, collected in Annex D. They concern
authorisation of canal openings, collective pumping, land lessees and night-time gate opening, the status of a village water
official and of customary wells, a village pipeline without a register entry, the binding force of
the minimum release, a utility's restriction on canal water, and sand extraction. This report does
not answer them.
